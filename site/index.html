It sounds robotic, and it invents specs you don't have.

Store owners describe the same three failures in the same words. None of them is a model problem.

"It can't write in my tone of voice." "Sounds so corporate and generic." And the expensive one — it invents product features that don't exist. Shopify's own Sidekick does it: one merchant reports it "kept making up products that didn't exist" (r/shopify, Dec 2025); another, that it invents product features in their images. A third asked for a month's sales and was told 3, against a real figure of 2,463. The complaints run through Feb 2026.

So you rewrite it yourself, which is the thing you were trying to avoid. One merchant put the economics plainly: if a tool costs more time auditing basic errors than it saves, it isn't a tool.

Why it happens

A model given a thin brief fills the gaps with an average of everything it has read about your category. Generic input, generic output. Average copy is the default, not a malfunction.

Which is why a bigger list of prompts doesn't help. You can get 1,000 of them for $19, or 100 for free. Nobody is short of prompts. The fix is closing the three gaps that produce generic writing: who specifically buys, what they're comparing you to, and what you can actually prove — and then never letting the model fill a gap silently again.

What this is

19 prompts and 4 workflows for running a store's copy, ads, email and conversion work. Every prompt takes a structured brief, refuses to invent facts, and ends with a self-check:

List every claim you made that I gave you no evidence for. Then list any sentence that would still make sense with a competitor's name in it.

That second question is the one that matters. Those sentences describe the category, not your product — and they're the exact texture people mean when they say copy "sounds AI-written."

What it won't do

It won't run your store while you sleep, and it won't 10x your conversion rate. And it won't make AI-written copy beat what you already have. The largest controlled comparison I can actually point to is outside ecommerce — 74,000 B2B cold emails split-tested over six months, where the human-written versions got 3.4% positive replies against the AI versions' 2.1%. For ecommerce email flows specifically I have no good evidence either way, and at least one merchant report runs the other direction. So: draft faster, stop inventing facts — but don't rip out flows that already work on the assumption the AI ones will win.
